Movement

Aerobic exercise and fitness

Cardiorespiratory fitness, how well your heart and lungs deliver oxygen, is one of the strongest known predictors of how long people live, and regular aerobic exercise is how you build it. The largest gain comes from moving from doing nothing to doing something.

Evidence AStrong Last reviewed 2026-10-24 7 min read

At a glance

  • The dose: 150–300 minutes a week of moderate activity, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity, or a mix. Any amount beats none.1
  • What the studies link it to: in 34 cohort studies, people with the highest fitness had about half the risk of death of those with the lowest, and the middle group already had about two-thirds.3
  • It also does things you can measure: lowers blood pressure by about 4.5/2.5 mmHg,5 lowers HbA1c in type 2 diabetes,6 and helps depression, though confidence in that evidence is low.7
  • Check first with unstable heart symptoms, heart failure that is not stable, severe uncontrolled high blood pressure, or an acute illness.

What the research shows

The World Health Organization recommends that adults do 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity or 75–150 minutes of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity a week, or an equivalent mix, plus regular muscle-strengthening. It also states that some activity is better than none and more is better still.1

In a pooled analysis of 94 cohorts, people doing about 150 minutes a week of moderate-to-vigorous activity had 31% lower all-cause mortality than the least active. The gain was greatest between zero and that level, smaller up to about twice that, and small and uncertain beyond.2

Fitness itself is an even stronger signal. In 34 cohort studies, each one-MET higher fitness (a unit of exertion: 1 MET is roughly the energy you use sitting at rest) went with 12% lower all-cause mortality. Compared with the lowest fitness group, the highest had about half the risk, and the intermediate group about two-thirds.3 An overview of 26 systematic reviews found the same pattern, but rated the certainty of the evidence from very low to moderate.4 Fitness reflects genes and general health as well as training, and these are observational data, so they show a strong link, not proof that exercise alone causes the difference.

Trials show effects you can measure. In 270 trials (15,827 people), aerobic training lowered resting blood pressure by about 4.5/2.5 mmHg, and combined aerobic and resistance training by about 6/2.5 mmHg (separate pooled estimates, not a head-to-head comparison).5 In type 2 diabetes, structured aerobic exercise lowered HbA1c by about 0.7 percentage points, and more than 150 minutes a week did better than 150 or less.6 In people with depression, walking or jogging gave a moderate benefit compared with active control conditions (for example usual care or a placebo tablet), larger at higher intensity, but only one included study was at low risk of bias, so confidence is low.7 In people with coronary heart disease, exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation programmes cut heart attacks by about 28% over 6–12 months (high-certainty evidence); the effect on all-cause mortality was borderline.8

Cardio and strength training are not interchangeable: the WHO recommends both. See the resistance training guide for the strength side.

How to do it

  1. Start where you are. Choose something you will actually repeat: brisk walking, cycling, swimming, jogging, dancing, the stairs. If you are inactive now, begin with a few short walks most days, as in the build-up plan below. The studies show the biggest gain per minute at the low end.
  2. Use the talk test for intensity. Moderate: you can talk but not sing. Vigorous: you can say only a few words before you need a breath.
  3. Build toward 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, spread over most days. Ten-minute bouts count.
  4. Keep most of it easy to moderate. Add a little harder work only once you have a base.
  5. Raise the total gradually, and start below what you think you can do. If joints complain, switch to low-impact options such as cycling, swimming, rowing or incline walking.
  6. Add two short strength sessions a week, and keep walking in daily life as well. See the daily steps guide.
  7. If you take blood pressure medicine (especially beta-blockers), judge effort by the talk test and how you feel, not by heart rate, and stand up slowly after you finish.

A twelve-week build-up

This plan is for people starting from inactivity. If you are already active, skip to the step that matches what you do now. If a step feels hard, repeat the previous one for another two weeks.

WeeksWhat to do
1–23 days a week, 15 minutes of easy walking, cycling or swimming. About 45 minutes in total.
3–44 days a week, 15 minutes, easy to moderate. About 60 minutes in total.
5–64 days a week, 20 minutes at a moderate pace. About 80 minutes in total.
7–85 days a week, 20 minutes at a moderate pace. About 100 minutes in total.
9–105 days a week, 25 minutes at a moderate pace. About 125 minutes in total.
11–125 days a week, 30 minutes at a moderate pace: 150 minutes in total.

Optional, only if you feel well and have no symptoms: once you are comfortable, change one session to brisk with bursts. Warm up for 5 minutes, then do 4 rounds of 1 minute faster (hard enough that you can say only a few words) and 2 minutes easy, then cool down for 5 minutes.

That is 150 minutes of aerobic activity and two strength sessions. Adjust the days to your life. This is where the build-up ends: do not start here from inactivity.

Who should be careful

Check with your doctor first if

  • You have chest pain or pressure, fainting, an uncontrolled irregular heartbeat or other new heart symptoms. Get them evaluated before you start this plan.
  • You have heart failure. Get your doctor's go-ahead first, especially if it has recently got worse or changed.
  • You have a fever or an acute illness. Wait until you have recovered.
  • Your blood pressure is severely high and not under control. Do not start until your doctor has cleared you and your blood pressure is being treated; then begin light.
  • You have coronary heart disease or have had a heart attack. Start in a supervised cardiac rehabilitation programme.
  • You take insulin or a sulfonylurea. Exercise can cause low blood sugar; talk to your doctor about adjusting medicine and carbohydrate, and check your glucose.
  • You are pregnant. Moderate aerobic activity is recommended, but avoid overheating, lying flat late in pregnancy, and activities with a high risk of falling, and check with your own clinician.
  • You have advanced diabetic eye disease (proliferative retinopathy) or advanced nerve damage. Limit high-impact and high-intensity work, and check your feet after each session.
  • You are unsure, or you have known heart, lung or kidney disease or another serious condition. Ask your doctor before starting.

Stop and get urgent help if

  • You get chest pain or pressure, you faint, or you are breathless out of proportion to the effort. Stop at once. For fainting, or chest pain or pressure that is new or does not ease within a few minutes of stopping, call emergency services (112 in the EU, 911 in the US, or your local emergency number). For breathlessness, call if it is severe or does not settle at rest.

Build up slowly, and stop if you feel sharp pain.

Common mistakes

  • Going too hard too soon. Soreness or injury in week two is the usual reason people quit.
  • Thinking you have no time. The minimum is small and splits into 10-minute bouts.
  • Counting steps as a substitute for any effort that raises your heart rate. Steps help, and so does brisk activity; see the daily steps guide.
  • Skipping strength training. Cardio and strength do different jobs.
  • Chasing intervals before you have an aerobic base.

How to tell it is working

Fitness is the headline measure. A lab test (VO2max) is best, but an estimate from a wearable or a simple timed test, such as a 12-minute distance test, repeated over months, is useful. Smaller signs: the same pace feels easier, your resting heart rate drifts down, and blood pressure, HbA1c or fasting glucose, waist and your weekly active minutes move in the right direction.

How strong is the evidence

Grade A. Large pooled analyses of cohorts consistently link both activity and fitness with lower mortality, trials show real improvements in blood pressure and HbA1c, and a major guideline sets the dose. The limits: the mortality evidence is observational (no trial can run to death), fitness partly reflects genes and health, the depression evidence has low confidence, and the blood-pressure effect is modest.

How I grade the evidence

Every guide carries a grade. It describes how well the research supports the advice, not how important the advice is. Grades come from a written rubric and are re-checked as new studies appear.

  • AStrongConsistent results from high-quality meta-analyses or several trials, a clear size of benefit, well-understood risks.
  • BModerateAt least one good systematic review or several solid trials. Promising, with real uncertainty about dose, groups or the long term.
  • CLimited but plausibleSome human evidence, but small, mixed or specific to one condition. Reasonable and low-risk, not proven.

Grade D (animal, mechanistic or influencer-driven claims) and E (disproven or unsafe) are not published here.

This is general information, not personal medical advice

It summarises published research for adults in general. It does not know your diagnoses, medicines or history. If you have a medical condition, take regular medication or are pregnant, check with your own doctor before changing what you eat or how you train, and follow the plan you were given if it differs from this page.
