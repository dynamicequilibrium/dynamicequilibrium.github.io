Sleep

Sleep regularity and duration

Both how long you sleep and how regularly you sleep predict health, and regularity may matter more. One of the most useful first steps is also the simplest: a fixed wake time, every day of the week.

Evidence AStrong Last reviewed 2026-10-14 4 min read

At a glance

  • Target: 7–9 hours in a consistent window; the wake time is the anchor, plus or minus 30 minutes, weekends included.
  • Why timing matters: in 61,000 mostly middle-aged and older UK adults, those in the four more regular fifths had 20–48% lower mortality than the least regular fifth, and regularity predicted it better than duration.3
  • Best for: short sleepers, shift workers, irregular schedules, people with prediabetes, high blood pressure or weight goals.
  • See a doctor, rather than adding hours in bed, if you snore loudly, wake unrefreshed after long sleep, have insomnia, or have bipolar disorder.

What the research shows

Sleep duration follows a U-shape: risk of death and cardiovascular events rises when you sleep less than about 7 hours, and also when you regularly sleep much more.1 Long sleep is partly a sign of illness rather than its cause. A joint consensus statement of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society says that adults should sleep at least 7 hours on a regular basis.2

Regularity adds something duration cannot. In 60,977 mostly middle-aged and older UK adults whose sleep was measured with wrist devices, those in the four more regular fifths had 20–48% lower mortality than the least regular fifth (the most regular fifth had the largest gap), and regularity predicted mortality better than how long people slept.3 This is observational, so it cannot show that regular sleep causes the lower risk.

The cause-and-effect evidence comes from trials in people who sleep too little. Short sleepers who extended their sleep by about 1.2 hours ate roughly 270 kcal a day less over two weeks.4 A very small trial in 18 men found that sleeping about 79 minutes longer improved insulin sensitivity and blood pressure; treat that as a hint only.5

Benefit concentrates on correcting too little or too erratic sleep. Pushing an already adequate sleeper towards very long sleep is not protective.

How to do it

  1. Fix your wake time. Same time every day, weekends included, within 30 minutes. It is easier to control than bedtime, and it pulls the rest of the day into line.
  2. Let bedtime follow sleepiness, while protecting a window of at least 7 hours.
  3. Light in the morning. Get outdoor light within 30–60 minutes of waking. Keep evening light dim.
  4. Caffeine early. Stop about 8–10 hours before bed.
  5. Go easy on alcohol near bedtime. It fragments the second half of the night.
  6. Keep naps short and early so they do not undermine night sleep.
  7. If you work shifts, keep the most regular schedule the job allows, and use bright light and short planned naps around the night shifts.

A two-week routine

DaysFocus
1–3Choose a wake time and keep it, even after a bad night. Open the curtains or step outside right away.
4–7Move your last coffee earlier. Dim the lights an hour before bed.
8–10Set a simple wind-down: a shower, a book, no phone in bed.
11–14Close the weekend gap: no more than 30 minutes later than weekdays.

A sample evening

21:30 Dim lights, phone on charge outside the bedroom or on the other side of the room.

22:00 Warm shower, tidy for tomorrow, jot down tomorrow's three tasks to clear your head.

22:30 Read something calm in bed. Lights out when you feel sleepy.

06:45 Alarm. Curtains open, water, ten minutes outside.

Who should get advice first

Talk to a clinician before changing your sleep if

  • You have bipolar disorder. Reduced sleep can trigger mania; changes to sleep should be supervised.
  • You have insomnia. More time in bed usually makes it worse. The effective treatment is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), not a longer sleep window.
  • You may have sleep apnoea (loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, unrefreshing sleep, resistant high blood pressure). It needs diagnosis and treatment.
  • You sleep more than 9 hours and still feel tired. That is worth a medical check (mood, thyroid, anaemia, apnoea, heart), not more sleep.

How to tell it is working

The best measure is consistency: bed and wake times that vary by less than about an hour from day to day, and a small gap between weekdays and weekends. A good wearable can show it, but a notebook works too. Resting heart rate, blood pressure, energy and mood are worth watching over weeks.

Common mistakes

  • Sleeping little on weekdays and catching up at the weekend. Total hours may balance, but regularity does not.
  • Treating time in bed as sleep.
  • Late caffeine and alcohol as "wind-down".
  • Chasing a perfect night. Aim for a pattern; one bad night does not matter.

How strong is the evidence

Grade A. Guideline-backed, with consistent large observational studies and supporting trials in people who sleep too little. The mortality findings are observational, since a trial to death is not possible. Because the benefit is greatest for correcting short or irregular sleep, it applies most to people in that group.

How I grade the evidence

Every guide carries a grade. It describes how well the research supports the advice, not how important the advice is. Grades come from a written rubric and are re-checked as new studies appear.

  • AStrongConsistent results from high-quality meta-analyses or several trials, a clear size of benefit, well-understood risks.
  • BModerateAt least one good systematic review or several solid trials. Promising, with real uncertainty about dose, groups or the long term.
  • CLimited but plausibleSome human evidence, but small, mixed or specific to one condition. Reasonable and low-risk, not proven.

Grade D (animal, mechanistic or influencer-driven claims) and E (disproven or unsafe) are not published here.

This is general information, not personal medical advice

It summarises published research for adults in general. It does not know your diagnoses, medicines or history. If you have a medical condition, take regular medication or are pregnant, check with your own doctor before changing what you eat or how you train, and follow the plan you were given if it differs from this page.
