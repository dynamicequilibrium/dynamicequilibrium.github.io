Habits

Cutting back on alcohol

Cutting back on alcohol is subtraction: you are removing a known carcinogen that also raises blood pressure, not adding a health habit. The belief that a daily glass is good for the heart has not held up, the biggest and most certain gains go to people who drink the most, and for light drinkers the plain message is that less is safer.

Evidence AStrong Last reviewed 2026-10-24 8 min read

At a glance

  • The message: alcohol is a Group 1 carcinogen, and the evidence shows no threshold below which its cancer effect disappears.1 For light drinkers, less is safer; for heavy drinkers, cutting down pays off the most.
  • Blood pressure: cutting down lowered it in people drinking more than two drinks a day, by about 5.5 mmHg systolic in those drinking six or more who halved their intake. At two a day or fewer there was no significant change.5
  • The heart: the apparent heart benefit of a daily glass shrank after adjusting for lifestyle, and genetic studies point to more hypertension and coronary disease with higher intake.6
  • Do not stop or sharply cut down on your own if you drink every day or most days, drink in the morning, shake, or have ever had withdrawal, delirium tremens or a seizure: it can cause seizures and delirium tremens and can be fatal. See a doctor first. If you are pregnant, the amount is zero.

What the research shows

A World Health Organization statement says alcohol is a Group 1 carcinogen (the highest category) and that the evidence does not show a threshold below which its cancer effect disappears, so no safe amount can be established.1 It is a short position statement, not a systematic review, but it points the same way as the analyses below.

A modelling study estimated that about 741,300 new cancers worldwide in 2020, 4.1% of all cases, were attributable to alcohol. Most came from heavy drinking (over 60 g of alcohol a day, 346,400 cases) and risky drinking (20–60 g, 291,800 cases), and 103,100 from moderate drinking under 20 g a day, roughly one and a half US standard drinks. About 41,300 of those were at up to 10 g a day, about one drink.2 The risk therefore starts at low intake, but most of the burden sits at the top.

The Global Burden of Disease analyses weigh all the harms and benefits together. The 2016 analysis found that the level of drinking that minimises overall health loss is zero.3 The 2020 analysis shows that it depends on age and region. In people aged 15–39 the lowest-risk intake was between zero and about 0.6 drinks a day. In people 40 and over the curve was J-shaped, with the lowest-risk intake between about 0.1 and 1.9 drinks a day depending on region (the Global Burden of Disease analyses use a standard drink of about 10 g of alcohol).4 The central estimate for older adults was under two drinks a day in every region, and nothing here says to start drinking.

Cutting down has a direct, measurable effect on blood pressure. In 36 trials (2,865 people), people who drank more than two drinks a day and cut back lowered their blood pressure, and the effect grew with how much they had been drinking. The largest drop, about 5.5/4.0 mmHg, was in people drinking six or more a day who roughly halved their intake. At two drinks a day or fewer there was no significant change.5

What about the daily glass of wine?

The idea that a daily glass protects the heart came mostly from observational studies, in which light drinkers also tend to have healthier lifestyles, and in which some people who had quit because of illness were counted as abstainers. In 371,463 UK adults, adjusting for lifestyle factors reduced the apparent protection. Mendelian randomisation, a genetic method that is less prone to that confounding, linked higher intake to more hypertension and coronary disease. Light intake was associated with only a minimal increase in risk and heavier intake with a steep one, and the authors concluded that alcohol of all amounts was associated with increased cardiovascular risk.6 Some burden models still show a small region of lower risk in older adults, as above, and nothing in this evidence supports drinking to protect your heart. It is a reason to be relaxed about one drink, not a reason to drink for your health.

Before you start: check this first

If any of these applies, skip the plan below and talk to a doctor first

  • You drink every day, or most days, over weeks or months, or you drink to avoid feeling unwell.
  • You drink in the morning, your hands shake, or you have ever had withdrawal symptoms (sweating, tremor, anxiety, nausea, seeing or hearing things).
  • You have ever had delirium tremens, a withdrawal seizure or any seizure. This includes a seizure disorder of any cause, even if you do not think you are dependent.
  • You are not sure whether this describes you. Treat it as if it does.

Stopping suddenly, or cutting down sharply, when your body is physically dependent can cause withdrawal seizures and delirium tremens, which can be fatal. A dry month on your own is not safe for you. Withdrawal should be supervised by a doctor or an addiction service. Dependence changes the route, not the goal: the aim is still to drink less, with medical help to get there safely.

How to do it

This plan is for people who are not physically dependent.

  1. Count what you drink. Write down what you drink for a week, in standard drinks. A standard drink holds about 14 g of alcohol in the US (12 oz of beer, 5 oz of wine, 1.5 oz of spirits), and often 8–12 g elsewhere, so check your local definition. Home and restaurant pours are often 1.5–2 times a standard drink. You can add the three-question AUDIT-C screening test; if it shows risky drinking, or you are unsure how to read it, talk to a doctor.
  2. Set a limit, and treat it as a ceiling. A common national upper limit is no more than about one drink a day for women and two for men, with several alcohol-free days a week. That is where much guidance sits, not a level shown to be risk-free: the cancer figures above include drinking in that range (two drinks a day falls in the 'risky' band). It does not apply to pregnancy, to dependence or to the groups in the boxes. Lower is safer.
  3. Schedule alcohol-free days in advance, so they are decided before the evening starts.
  4. Don't bank the week. Saving a week's drinks for the weekend raises the risk of injury, irregular heartbeat and high blood pressure even if the weekly average looks moderate.
  5. Change the environment. Don't keep it at home, have a good non-alcoholic option ready, and plan what you will order or say in social settings.
  6. Try a 4–8 week experiment of reduced drinking and measure what changes: home blood pressure, sleep, weight and, with your doctor, liver enzymes (GGT, ALT) and triglycerides. If you are drinking daily, do not turn it into a dry stretch without medical advice.
  7. Expect different timelines. Sleep and blood pressure often respond within weeks. Liver enzymes and weight take one to three months.

A four-week reduction (for people who are not physically dependent)

WeekWhat you do
1Count every drink and its real size. Check your home blood pressure on a few mornings. No change yet.
2Choose three alcohol-free days and keep them. On drinking days, set a cap before you start.
3Move to four or five alcohol-free days, or lower your cap. Keep a non-alcoholic drink ready for evenings.
4Check blood pressure and sleep again and compare with week 1. Decide your next step: keep this level, or go lower.

Who should be careful

Check with your doctor first if

  • You are pregnant, or trying to become pregnant. No amount of alcohol is known to be safe. The goal is zero, not reduction. If you are also drinking heavily or every day, get medical help to stop safely rather than stopping abruptly on your own.
  • You take medicines. Alcohol interacts with sedatives and benzodiazepines, opioids, sleep aids, some antidepressants, acetaminophen (paracetamol, a liver risk), warfarin and other anticoagulants, and diabetes medicines (it makes blood sugar more variable). Cutting back usually helps, but tell your prescriber or pharmacist and do not change your medicines yourself.

Get urgent medical help if

  • After cutting down or stopping you have severe shaking, confusion, hallucinations, a seizure, fever, repeated vomiting, or a racing heart with heavy sweating. These can be signs of dangerous withdrawal. Symptoms can start within hours of the last drink and get worse over the next few days, so do not wait to see if they pass. Do not be alone, do not drive yourself, and call emergency services (112 in the EU, 911 in the US, or your local emergency number).

If you cannot cut down despite trying, that is a medical problem, not a character flaw. Talk to a doctor about help.

Common mistakes

  • Believing a daily glass of red wine is protective, or drinking for your heart.
  • Guessing the count. Pours are usually bigger than a standard drink.
  • Saving the week's drinks for the weekend.
  • Stopping suddenly, or cutting down sharply, after heavy daily drinking.
  • Relying on willpower alone, instead of changing what is in the house and what you do in the evenings.
  • Berating yourself after a lapse. One lapse does not cancel progress; look at the weekly pattern.

How to tell it is working

Track your weekly count of standard drinks. Measure home blood pressure (average several readings) before and after, and watch sleep quality, energy and weight. With your doctor, GGT and ALT/AST, triglycerides and MCV can show the change; MCV and GGT also reflect sustained drinking. Trends over weeks matter more than any single reading.

How strong is the evidence

Grade A. The direction of the harm is consistent across designs and statements: cancer risk and blood pressure rise with intake, overall health loss rises with intake apart from a small region of lower risk in some older adults, and trials show that cutting down lowers blood pressure in people who drink more than two drinks a day. The limits: no trial can run to death or cancer, the cancer numbers are modelled estimates, the blood pressure trials found no effect at two drinks a day or fewer, and for light drinkers the absolute risk is small, so the case for them rests on caution, not on a large proven gain.

How I grade the evidence

Every guide carries a grade. It describes how well the research supports the advice, not how important the advice is. Grades come from a written rubric and are re-checked as new studies appear.

  • AStrongConsistent results from high-quality meta-analyses or several trials, a clear size of benefit, well-understood risks.
  • BModerateAt least one good systematic review or several solid trials. Promising, with real uncertainty about dose, groups or the long term.
  • CLimited but plausibleSome human evidence, but small, mixed or specific to one condition. Reasonable and low-risk, not proven.

Grade D (animal, mechanistic or influencer-driven claims) and E (disproven or unsafe) are not published here.

This is general information, not personal medical advice

It summarises published research for adults in general. It does not know your diagnoses, medicines or history. If you have a medical condition, take regular medication or are pregnant, check with your own doctor before changing what you eat or how you train, and follow the plan you were given if it differs from this page.
