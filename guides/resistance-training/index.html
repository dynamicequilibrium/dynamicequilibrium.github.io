Movement

Resistance training

Strength training does more than build muscle: people who do it have lower risk of death, heart disease, cancer and diabetes. The dose that gives most of the benefit is small, about 30 to 60 minutes a week.

Evidence AStrong Last reviewed 2026-10-14 3 min read

At a glance

  • Minimum: two full-body sessions a week of 20–30 minutes, one or two sets per exercise.
  • Standard: two or three sessions a week, all major muscle groups, gradually adding load.
  • What the studies link it to: roughly 10–17% lower risk of death, heart disease, cancer and diabetes, with most benefit at 30–60 minutes a week.1
  • Check first if you have uncontrolled high blood pressure, a recent heart event, severe frailty, or take insulin.

What the research shows

A pooled analysis of cohort studies found that people who do muscle-strengthening activity have about 10–17% lower risk of death, cardiovascular disease, cancer and diabetes. The association was strongest at 30–60 minutes a week and did not increase further after that.1 A second analysis found about 15% lower all-cause mortality, and the greatest reduction in people who also did aerobic exercise.2

In trials, strength training improves strength, muscle mass, insulin sensitivity, function and mood, and modestly lowers blood pressure. The mortality figures are from observational studies, because trials cannot run to death, but the pattern is consistent and the dose is small.

How to do it

  1. Two sessions a week to start, full body, 20–30 minutes.
  2. Choose 5–6 big movements: a squat or sit-to-stand, a hinge (hip bridge or deadlift), a push, a pull, a carry or plank.
  3. Do 1–2 sets of 8–12 repetitions that leave 2–3 reps in reserve.
  4. Progress. When you can do the top number of reps with good form, add a little weight, a harder variation or one more set.
  5. Rest 48 hours before training the same muscle group again.
  6. Eat enough protein to support the work.

Who should be careful

Check with your doctor first if

  • Your blood pressure is uncontrolled. Avoid heavy lifts with breath-holding until it is controlled.
  • You have had a recent heart attack or cardiac event. Start in a rehabilitation programme.
  • You are very frail. Train with supervision.
  • You have tendon or joint problems. Reduce the load and have the technique checked.
  • You have back pain, osteoporosis, recent surgery, or you are pregnant. Get advice on which exercises suit you.
  • You take insulin or a sulfonylurea. Training can cause low blood sugar; tell your doctor before you start.

Stop if you feel sharp pain.

Stop and get urgent help if

  • You get chest pain or pressure, fainting, or breathlessness out of proportion to the effort. Stop, and call emergency services (112 in the EU, 911 in the US) if it is severe or does not settle at rest.

Teenagers can train safely with good technique and supervision. No gym is needed: bodyweight, bands and household weights are enough to start.

Common mistakes

  • Lifting for ego instead of form.
  • Changing the programme every week, so nothing progresses.
  • Skipping the legs.
  • Assuming harder is better. A session that leaves you wrecked is not more effective.
  • Using walking as a substitute. Walking and strength are complementary, not interchangeable. See the daily steps guide.

How to tell it is working

Weights or reps going up over weeks, easier stairs and getting up from a chair, and grip strength, a simple measure linked to long-term health. Waist, blood pressure and HbA1c can also improve.

How strong is the evidence

Grade A. Consistent analyses link muscle-strengthening with lower mortality and disease, and trials show clear benefits for strength and metabolism. The dose is characterised and the safety profile is good. The mortality evidence is observational, so it shows a strong association rather than proving cause.

How I grade the evidence

Every guide carries a grade. It describes how well the research supports the advice, not how important the advice is. Grades come from a written rubric and are re-checked as new studies appear.

  • AStrongConsistent results from high-quality meta-analyses or several trials, a clear size of benefit, well-understood risks.
  • BModerateAt least one good systematic review or several solid trials. Promising, with real uncertainty about dose, groups or the long term.
  • CLimited but plausibleSome human evidence, but small, mixed or specific to one condition. Reasonable and low-risk, not proven.

Grade D (animal, mechanistic or influencer-driven claims) and E (disproven or unsafe) are not published here.

This is general information, not personal medical advice

It summarises published research for adults in general. It does not know your diagnoses, medicines or history. If you have a medical condition, take regular medication or are pregnant, check with your own doctor before changing what you eat or how you train, and follow the plan you were given if it differs from this page.
