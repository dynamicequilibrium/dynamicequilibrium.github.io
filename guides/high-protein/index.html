Nutrition

Higher-protein eating

Eating more protein than the bare minimum helps you keep lean mass while you lose weight, and helps training build strength and lean mass. The effects are real but modest, a few tenths of a kilogram of lean mass and under a kilogram of extra weight loss, and they show up in people who are already dieting or lifting. It is a useful adjunct, not a fat-loss drug.

Evidence AStrong Last reviewed 2026-10-24 6 min read

At a glance

  • Target: roughly 1.2–1.6 g of protein per kg of body weight a day if you are dieting or training (the usual minimum for adults is 0.8). Older adults need at least 1.0–1.2.5
  • How big: while dieting, about 0.8 kg more weight lost, 0.9 kg more fat lost and 0.4 kg less fat-free mass lost over roughly 12 weeks, compared with a standard-protein diet.2
  • With lifting: protein supplements added to training gave about 0.3 kg more fat-free mass, and the gain stopped rising beyond a total intake of about 1.6 g/kg a day.1
  • Kidneys: in the trials reviewed, higher protein did not change kidney filtration in healthy adults,4 but that does not extend to kidney disease. Check first with any known kidney disease, severe liver disease or a rare protein-metabolism disorder.

What the research shows

Most of the evidence comes from two situations: people adding resistance training, and people dieting to lose weight.

In 49 trials (1,863 people) that added protein supplements to resistance training, supplements gave about 0.3 kg more fat-free mass and about 2.5 kg more strength on a one-repetition-maximum lift. The gain in fat-free mass was smaller with increasing age and larger in people who were already trained, and it stopped rising once total protein intake passed about 1.6 g per kg of body weight a day.1 These were trials of supplements, so they do not show that supplements beat the same protein from food.

In people dieting to lose weight, 24 trials (1,063 adults, about 12 weeks on average) compared high-protein with standard-protein diets of the same energy and fat content. High protein gave about 0.8 kg more weight loss (95% CI 0.1–1.5 kg), 0.9 kg more fat loss (0.5–1.3 kg) and 0.4 kg less fat-free mass lost (0.1–0.8 kg). Fullness was greater with high protein in 3 of the 5 studies that measured it.2 In 20 trials of adults over 50 who lost weight, those eating more protein kept more lean mass (about 0.8 kg more when higher protein meant at least 1.0 g/kg a day, and about 0.5 kg more when it meant at least 25% of energy) and lost somewhat more fat.3

Read the size honestly: these are fractions of a kilogram to about a kilogram over three months. Protein helps most as a supporting measure alongside training and a controlled energy intake. The evidence is strongest for body composition, strength and fullness over weeks to months. Long-term data on disease or mortality are limited.

On kidneys, 28 trials (1,358 healthy adults without kidney disease) found that higher protein did not change kidney filtration rate (GFR). The trials only had to last more than four days, and the review flagged an unclear risk of selection bias, so this says nothing about decades of high intake. It is reassuring for people with healthy kidneys. It does not extend to people who already have kidney disease.4 For older people (over 65), an expert position paper recommends at least 1.0–1.2 g per kg a day, 1.2 or more if they exercise, and 1.2–1.5 with acute or chronic disease, with severe kidney disease as the exception.5

How to do it

  1. Work out your target. Multiply your weight in kg by 1.2–1.6. A 70 kg person aims for about 85–110 g a day. If you are dieting and want to protect lean mass, go toward the upper end; a practical range for a steep deficit is up to about 2.0 g/kg (a convention, not a trial result). The 1.6 g/kg plateau above comes from the lifting trials. Older adults should aim for at least 1.0–1.2 g/kg, and more if they train or are ill.
  2. Spread it over 3–4 meals, roughly 0.3–0.4 g per kg per meal, which is about 20–40 g for most adults (closer to 21–28 g at 70 kg). This is a common practical convention; the trials above did not test it.
  3. Get it from food first: lean meat, fish, eggs, dairy, legumes and soy. Powders (whey, casein, plant blends) are a convenience, not a requirement.
  4. Pair it with resistance training. That is what turns the extra protein into muscle. See the resistance training guide.
  5. Keep the rest of your plate. Vegetables, fruit and whole grains still matter, and going far beyond about 1.6–2.2 g/kg adds cost and crowds out other foods without added benefit.
  6. Drink enough fluid.
  7. Count the grams for a week to see what you actually eat.

That is about 111 g, with meals toward the upper end of the range. Plant-based swaps: 150 g firm tofu (about 15–20 g), 150 g cooked lentils (about 13 g) or 30 g of soy or pea protein powder (about 20–25 g). Protein values are approximate and vary by product, so check labels. They come from standard food tables, not from the studies cited here.

Who should be careful

Check with your doctor first if

  • You have any known kidney disease or a reduced kidney filtration rate (eGFR), especially advanced chronic kidney disease (stage 4–5), or you follow a protein-restricted renal diet. Do not raise your protein without guidance from a nephrologist. The reassuring kidney data are for healthy adults.
  • You have severe liver disease or hepatic encephalopathy. This needs medical supervision.
  • You have a rare inborn disorder of protein or amino-acid metabolism. This needs medical supervision.
  • You have diabetes and take medicine for it. Dieting and changing what you eat can change how much medicine you need. Coordinate with your clinician and do not adjust doses on your own.
  • You are pregnant. You need enough protein but not extreme amounts; ask your clinician for an individual plan.
  • You are unsure of your kidney function, are over 65, or have long-standing diabetes or high blood pressure. Ask for a kidney blood test (eGFR) before you raise protein substantially, and repeat it periodically. With diabetes or high blood pressure, ask for a urine albumin test as well (standard practice, beyond these studies).
  • You have, or have had, an eating disorder. Do not use this page to diet; talk to a clinician.

Common mistakes

  • Expecting a fat-loss miracle. The effect is modest and depends on a calorie deficit and, for muscle, on training.
  • Eating most of the protein at one meal instead of spreading it.
  • Replacing vegetables and fibre with shakes and bars.
  • Pushing far beyond about 1.6–2.2 g/kg for no added benefit, at extra cost.
  • Raising protein sharply with kidney disease, or without knowing your kidney function when you have risk factors.

How to tell it is working

Track body weight and waist, strength (grip strength or your main lifts) and, if you can, body composition (DEXA or repeated bioimpedance). Notice whether hunger is easier to manage while dieting. Log your daily protein grams to confirm you are actually eating the amount you planned. If you have kidney risk factors, track eGFR with your doctor.

How strong is the evidence

Grade A. Several meta-analyses of randomised trials agree that more protein helps keep lean mass during weight loss, adds to strength and lean-mass gains from training, and improves fullness, and expert guidance supports higher intakes for older adults. The honest limits: the effects are modest in each analysis, the lifting trials tested supplements added to training, the dieting trials lasted about 12 weeks on average, long-term hard-outcome data are limited, and the kidney reassurance applies to healthy kidneys only.

How I grade the evidence

Every guide carries a grade. It describes how well the research supports the advice, not how important the advice is. Grades come from a written rubric and are re-checked as new studies appear.

  • AStrongConsistent results from high-quality meta-analyses or several trials, a clear size of benefit, well-understood risks.
  • BModerateAt least one good systematic review or several solid trials. Promising, with real uncertainty about dose, groups or the long term.
  • CLimited but plausibleSome human evidence, but small, mixed or specific to one condition. Reasonable and low-risk, not proven.

Grade D (animal, mechanistic or influencer-driven claims) and E (disproven or unsafe) are not published here.

This is general information, not personal medical advice

It summarises published research for adults in general. It does not know your diagnoses, medicines or history. If you have a medical condition, take regular medication or are pregnant, check with your own doctor before changing what you eat or how you train, and follow the plan you were given if it differs from this page.
