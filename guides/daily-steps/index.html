Movement

Daily steps and walking

Walking more is among the best-supported and lowest-risk things you can do for long-term health. You do not need 10,000 steps: most of the benefit arrives much earlier.

Evidence AStrong Last reviewed 2026-10-14 4 min read

At a glance

  • Start here: about 4,000 steps a day is already clearly better than 2,000, and every extra 1,000 helps.1
  • Good target: about 7,000–8,000 a day; about 8,000–10,000 if you are under 60.2
  • Faster adds more: some brisk walking gives extra benefit beyond the number of steps.3
  • Check first after a recent heart event or with unstable angina, severe frailty, diabetes treated with insulin, or foot ulcers.

What the research shows

Researchers who pooled many studies found that people walking about 7,000 steps a day had roughly half the risk of dying during follow-up compared with people walking about 2,000 steps. Lower risks were also seen for cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, dementia, depression and falls.1

The benefit does not climb forever. It rises steeply at low levels and flattens out at about 6,000–8,000 steps a day for people aged 60 and over, and about 8,000–10,000 for younger adults.2 The famous 10,000 figure is not a threshold. If you now walk 3,000 steps, reaching 6,000 is a big win.

Pace matters too. In a UK study of about 78,500 people, faster walking was associated with extra benefit beyond the number of steps.3 These studies are observational, because no one can randomise people to a step count for years, but the results are consistent, dose-graded and plausible.

How to build it

  1. Measure first. Use your phone for 3 days and take the average. That is your baseline.
  2. Add gently. Raise your daily average by about 10–20% a week. Faster increases cause sore feet, shins and knees.
  3. Anchor it to something you already do: a call, a commute, the dog, the walk after a meal. A 10–15 minute walk after eating also blunts the rise in blood sugar.
  4. Split it up. Short walks count. The 2018 US guidelines dropped the old "10 minutes at a time" rule.4
  5. Add some brisk walking: a pace where you can talk but not sing.

A six-week plan from a low start (baseline about 3,000 steps)

The daily averages are targets, and the added walks are cumulative: each week keeps what you added before. If your baseline is already 6,000, skip ahead: aim for 7,000–8,000 and add brisk blocks.

What counts as progress

Use a seven-day average of your steps; one day tells you little. Resting heart rate, blood pressure, energy, mood and sleep are all worth watching alongside the step count. Use the same device over time, since wrist trackers are imprecise on absolute numbers but fine for trends.

Who should be careful

Get advice before increasing if

  • You have had a recent heart attack or have unstable angina. Build up under cardiac rehabilitation guidance.
  • You have heart failure or severe lung disease. Increase under your doctor's advice.
  • You have joint pain, an injury, or osteoarthritis. Use supportive shoes and softer surfaces; build slowly.
  • You take insulin or a sulfonylurea. More activity can cause low blood sugar; tell your doctor before you start.
  • You are frail or at high risk of falls. Walk with support or supervision.
  • You have diabetes with numb feet or foot ulcers. Check your feet daily and wear protective footwear.

Walking is generally encouraged during pregnancy unless your obstetrician has said otherwise.

Stop and get urgent help if

  • You get chest pain or pressure, fainting, or breathlessness out of proportion to the effort. Stop, and call emergency services (112 in the EU, 911 in the US) if it is severe or does not settle at rest.

Common mistakes

  • Treating 10,000 as pass or fail, then giving up at 5,000.
  • Using walking instead of strength training. They add to each other and do not replace each other.
  • Only strolling. A few brisk blocks add benefit.
  • Jumping up too fast and getting injured.

How strong is the evidence

Grade A. Large, consistent analyses of device-measured steps, a clear dose pattern and a well-understood safety profile. The main limitation is that the data are observational: people who walk more also tend to be healthier in other ways, although the studies try to account for that. A trial randomising people to walk for years is not practical.

How I grade the evidence

Every guide carries a grade. It describes how well the research supports the advice, not how important the advice is. Grades come from a written rubric and are re-checked as new studies appear.

  • AStrongConsistent results from high-quality meta-analyses or several trials, a clear size of benefit, well-understood risks.
  • BModerateAt least one good systematic review or several solid trials. Promising, with real uncertainty about dose, groups or the long term.
  • CLimited but plausibleSome human evidence, but small, mixed or specific to one condition. Reasonable and low-risk, not proven.

Grade D (animal, mechanistic or influencer-driven claims) and E (disproven or unsafe) are not published here.

This is general information, not personal medical advice

It summarises published research for adults in general. It does not know your diagnoses, medicines or history. If you have a medical condition, take regular medication or are pregnant, check with your own doctor before changing what you eat or how you train, and follow the plan you were given if it differs from this page.
