Nutrition

Time-restricted eating

Time-restricted eating means keeping all your food inside a fixed window each day. The evidence says it works mainly because most people eat less, not because of the clock. For some people it is a useful rule, and for others it is the wrong tool.

Evidence BModerate Last reviewed 2026-10-14 4 min read

At a glance

  • What it is: all meals within about 8–10 hours, for example 08:00–18:00; water, plain tea and black coffee outside it.
  • What to expect: a small loss of weight, no better than ordinary calorie reduction.1 A modest HbA1c improvement was seen in adults with metabolic syndrome on standard care.4
  • Best for: people who dislike counting calories and tend to graze late into the evening.
  • Not for: anyone with a history of an eating disorder, pregnancy, type 1 diabetes, insulin or sulfonylurea treatment, frailty or low body weight, or under 18.

What it is

You pick a window of 8 to 10 hours in which you eat, and stop eating outside it. Inside the window you eat normally. It is a behavioural rule that replaces counting calories, not a special metabolic state.

What the research shows

A 2025 analysis of 99 trials (6,582 adults) found that time-restricted eating gave small weight loss compared with eating freely, but no more than ordinary calorie reduction. Differences were small and mostly faded after about six months.1 A second analysis found the same: when calories are matched, the benefit of the window shrinks, so most of the effect is eating less.2

One well-known trial, TREAT, tested 16:8 in 116 people for 12 weeks without other changes. It showed no advantage over three regular meals, and lean mass fell.3 In contrast, a trial in 108 adults with metabolic syndrome who were already on standard care found a modest improvement in HbA1c and no loss of lean mass.4 Pooled trials show modest gains in weight, waist, fasting insulin and cholesterol.5

No trial has shown that it reduces heart attacks or deaths.

How to do it

  1. Choose a window of 8–10 hours, earlier in the day if you can (for example 08:00–18:00).
  2. Finish your last meal at least 3 hours before bed. Earlier windows are better supported than late ones.
  3. Outside the window: water, plain tea, black coffee.
  4. Protect your muscle. Spread protein across your meals (a commonly used target is roughly 1.2–1.6 g per kg of body weight a day; if you have kidney disease, ask your doctor first) and do strength training twice a week (see the resistance training guide).
  5. Keep the same window every day. Consistency matters more than the exact hours.

Example day (window 08:00–18:00)

08:00 Breakfast. Three eggs or a large pot of Greek yoghurt, wholemeal toast, fruit.

12:30 Lunch. Chicken breast or lentils with feta, a large portion of vegetables, whole grains.

15:30 Snack. Cottage cheese or a handful of nuts, an apple.

17:30 Dinner. Fish or tofu, vegetables, potatoes or rice. Kitchen closed at 18:00.

Aim for a solid portion of protein at every meal rather than one big serving in the evening. Two eggs or a small pot of yoghurt alone is usually not enough.

Who should not do it

Avoid it, or only do it with your doctor, if

  • You have ever had an eating disorder or disordered eating. Restriction can bring it back.
  • You are pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • You have type 1 diabetes, or take insulin, a sulfonylurea or any other diabetes medicine. Fasting can cause dangerous low blood sugar and the doses may need to change. Ask first.
  • You are underweight, frail or older with a risk of losing muscle.
  • You are under 18.
  • You take blood-pressure medicine. Pressure can fall further; ask your doctor to review it.
  • You work night shifts. Align the window with your actual waking hours.
  • You train hard or want to build muscle. Pair the window with enough protein and strength training, because of the lean-mass loss seen in one trial.
  • You have kidney disease. Ask before raising protein.

How to tell it is working

Follow your weight and waist, and, if you have prediabetes or metabolic syndrome, HbA1c and fasting glucose. If you are older or in a calorie deficit, watch strength, such as grip or how easily you stand from a chair, to catch muscle loss early.

Common mistakes

  • Treating the window as permission to eat anything inside it.
  • Skipping protein and strength work, then losing muscle along with fat.
  • Using a late window that ends close to bedtime.
  • Staying with it after it has begun to feel like punishment. If it does, it is the wrong tool.

How strong is the evidence

Grade B (moderate). There are many trials and a high-level analysis, but they show small effects that match, and do not beat, ordinary calorie restriction. Benefits fade over time, and there is no data on heart attacks or deaths. That is why this guide describes it as a simplifying rule, not a better metabolic strategy.

How I grade the evidence

Every guide carries a grade. It describes how well the research supports the advice, not how important the advice is. Grades come from a written rubric and are re-checked as new studies appear.

  • AStrongConsistent results from high-quality meta-analyses or several trials, a clear size of benefit, well-understood risks.
  • BModerateAt least one good systematic review or several solid trials. Promising, with real uncertainty about dose, groups or the long term.
  • CLimited but plausibleSome human evidence, but small, mixed or specific to one condition. Reasonable and low-risk, not proven.

Grade D (animal, mechanistic or influencer-driven claims) and E (disproven or unsafe) are not published here.

This is general information, not personal medical advice

It summarises published research for adults in general. It does not know your diagnoses, medicines or history. If you have a medical condition, take regular medication or are pregnant, check with your own doctor before changing what you eat or how you train, and follow the plan you were given if it differs from this page.
