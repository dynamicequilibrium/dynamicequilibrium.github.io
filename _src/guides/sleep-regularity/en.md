## What the research shows

Sleep duration follows a U-shape: risk of death and cardiovascular events rises when you sleep less than about 7 hours, and also when you regularly sleep much more.[^yin2017] Long sleep is partly a sign of illness rather than its cause. A joint consensus statement of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society says that adults should sleep at least 7 hours on a regular basis.[^watson2015]

Regularity adds something duration cannot. In 60,977 mostly middle-aged and older UK adults whose sleep was measured with wrist devices, those in the four more regular fifths had 20–48% lower mortality than the least regular fifth (the most regular fifth had the largest gap), and regularity predicted mortality better than how long people slept.[^windred2024] This is observational, so it cannot show that regular sleep causes the lower risk.

The cause-and-effect evidence comes from trials in people who sleep too little. Short sleepers who extended their sleep by about 1.2 hours ate roughly 270 kcal a day less over two weeks.[^tasali2022] A very small trial in 18 men found that sleeping about 79 minutes longer improved insulin sensitivity and blood pressure; treat that as a hint only.[^hartescu2022]

Benefit concentrates on correcting too little or too erratic sleep. Pushing an already adequate sleeper towards very long sleep is not protective.

## How to do it

1. **Fix your wake time.** Same time every day, weekends included, within 30 minutes. It is easier to control than bedtime, and it pulls the rest of the day into line.
2. **Let bedtime follow sleepiness,** while protecting a window of at least 7 hours.
3. **Light in the morning.** Get outdoor light within 30–60 minutes of waking. Keep evening light dim.
4. **Caffeine early.** Stop about 8–10 hours before bed.
5. **Go easy on alcohol near bedtime.** It fragments the second half of the night.
6. **Keep naps short and early** so they do not undermine night sleep.
7. **If you work shifts,** keep the most regular schedule the job allows, and use bright light and short planned naps around the night shifts.

:::plan A two-week routine
| Days | Focus |
|---|---|
| 1–3 | Choose a wake time and keep it, even after a bad night. Open the curtains or step outside right away. |
| 4–7 | Move your last coffee earlier. Dim the lights an hour before bed. |
| 8–10 | Set a simple wind-down: a shower, a book, no phone in bed. |
| 11–14 | Close the weekend gap: no more than 30 minutes later than weekdays. |
:::

:::plan A sample evening
**21:30** Dim lights, phone on charge outside the bedroom or on the other side of the room.

**22:00** Warm shower, tidy for tomorrow, jot down tomorrow's three tasks to clear your head.

**22:30** Read something calm in bed. Lights out when you feel sleepy.

**06:45** Alarm. Curtains open, water, ten minutes outside.
:::

## Who should get advice first

:::callout safety Talk to a clinician before changing your sleep if
- **You have bipolar disorder.** Reduced sleep can trigger mania; changes to sleep should be supervised.
- **You have insomnia.** More time in bed usually makes it worse. The effective treatment is cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), not a longer sleep window.
- **You may have sleep apnoea** (loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, unrefreshing sleep, resistant high blood pressure). It needs diagnosis and treatment.
- **You sleep more than 9 hours and still feel tired.** That is worth a medical check (mood, thyroid, anaemia, apnoea, heart), not more sleep.
:::

## How to tell it is working

The best measure is consistency: bed and wake times that vary by less than about an hour from day to day, and a small gap between weekdays and weekends. A good wearable can show it, but a notebook works too. Resting heart rate, blood pressure, energy and mood are worth watching over weeks.

## Common mistakes

- Sleeping little on weekdays and catching up at the weekend. Total hours may balance, but regularity does not.
- Treating time in bed as sleep.
- Late caffeine and alcohol as "wind-down".
- Chasing a perfect night. Aim for a pattern; one bad night does not matter.

## How strong is the evidence

**Grade A.** Guideline-backed, with consistent large observational studies and supporting trials in people who sleep too little. The mortality findings are observational, since a trial to death is not possible. Because the benefit is greatest for correcting short or irregular sleep, it applies most to people in that group.
