How I work

A short account of the thinking behind the consultations: what I look at, how I decide what matters, and where my role ends.

The starting point

I begin with what already exists: your history, your records, the medicines and supplements you take, what you have already tried. New tests come later, and only if the result could change what we do.

Principles

  • One system, not separate complaints. Sleep, energy, mood, hormones, relationships and meaning influence each other. I look for the thread that, if addressed first, eases the others.
  • Fewer things, done well. A plan with three steps you follow is better than one with thirty you abandon. I rank steps by likely impact and by what is realistic in your life.
  • Evidence, with its limits. I draw on published research and tell you how strong the evidence behind a suggestion is — and when it is thin.
  • Tests only when they change a decision. I try to avoid long panels and supplement lists that add cost without adding clarity.
  • Medicines stay with the prescriber. If a question touches a prescription, I help you put it to the doctor who prescribes it.
  • Honest about limits. I will say when something is outside what I can help with, and where to turn instead.

A consultation, step by step

1. Understand your situation

Usually before we meet, you fill in a written questionnaire covering your history, sleep, mood, stress and habits, and send what you already have: records, results, and a list of every medicine and supplement you take. We then talk it through.

2. Identify priorities

I read symptoms, diagnoses, results and history as a pattern rather than a list of numbers. As a physician I consider the possible explanations — including the ones that need a doctor’s attention — and say which look likely, which unlikely, and which would need an examination or a test to settle. I try to separate what is well established, what is suggested, and what is uncertain, and we choose what deserves your effort first — and what can safely wait.

3. Build an individual plan

You receive a written plan with a small number of actions, ordered by likely impact. It starts with the basics — sleep, light, movement, food, stress — before any supplements, and it marks what is worth discussing with your own doctors and which signs mean you should seek care promptly.

4. Review and adjust

We look at what happened. If a repeat test is useful, we decide in advance what question it answers. Plans change as your situation does.

Following progress

We agree beforehand what to watch: sleep, energy, mood, particular symptoms and, where it makes sense, repeated lab values. I sometimes use short questionnaires such as PHQ-9 and GAD-7 to track change over time; they are not diagnostic tools.

Where my role ends

This is consultative support, not a medical service. It sits alongside your own medical care. A consultation can give you a physician’s reading of your history, diagnoses and results, and a plan for what to do next. It does not replace formal diagnosis, treatment, prescribing or emergency care by your treating doctors, and it is not psychotherapy. I do not change treatment prescribed by other doctors; questions about medication are for the doctor who prescribes it, and I will help you put them clearly.

Some situations need a different kind of help first — new or severe symptoms, thoughts of self-harm, an acute crisis. I will tell you plainly when that is the case, and I may ask you to seek care before we continue. In an emergency, contact local emergency services (112 in the EU, 911 in the US).
